                                                                                         Abstract
                                                   We consider the online carpooling problem, where edges arrive online and must be oriented
                                               immediately while keeping the discrepancy between the indegree and outdegree at each vertex
                                               small. We prove that the natural Greedy algorithm incurs discrepancy O(min{T 1/3 , n}) after T
                                               arrivals. This resolves a question of Ajtai et al. [AAN+ 98], who showed that any deterministic
                                               algorithm must incur Ω(min{T 1/3 , n}) discrepancy, and gave an algorithm with O(min{T 1/2 , n})
                                               discrepancy.
                                                   We also show a similar square-root to cube-root improvement in the stochastic setting, where
                                               O(n) edges are sampled independently from an underlying n-vertex graph G. Formally, we show
                                               an O((log n)1/3 ) bound for random arrivals from any ∆-regular graph G. When ∆ = Ω((log n)3 ),
                                               we show the more refined bound of O((log n/ log ∆)1/3 + log log n) on the discrepancy. We
                                               show that the cube-root term in the previous bound is essential, while the log log n term is
                                               already known to be √ necessary for random arrivals from complete graphs. The previous upper
                                               bounds here were O( log n), which follow from the breakthrough works on online discrepancy
                                               [KRR24, AA26].
                                                   Our techniques for proving such cube-root-type bounds may be of independent interest,
                                               as the standard quadratic-potential and subgaussian analyses underlying the previous general
                                               bounds appear inherently unable to go below square-root-type guarantees.

1        Introduction

We consider the classical carpooling problem introduced by Fagin and Williams [FW83], and defined
as follows: We are given a set V of n nodes, and undirected edges arrive online. Upon arrival of an
edge (i, j), it has to be oriented immediately as either i → j or j → i. The goal is to minimize the
maximum imbalance between the in-degree and out-degree of any node. Formally, if δt− (i) and δt+ (i)
denote the number of in-edges and out-edges incident to i at time t, define the signed discrepancy
of node i as dt (i) := δt− (i) − δt+ (i). The goal is to minimize

                                            max max |dt (i)|.
                                             t     i

incoming vectors vt have the form 1(i) − 1(j) for i, j ∈ [n], where 1(i) denotes the i-th standard

incoming and outgoing weights.
Interestingly, despite its simple structure, the carpooling problem captures several more general
problems and phenomena. For example, Ajtai et al. [AAN+ 98] showed that it is equivalent, up to
a factor of 2, to general carpooling where arbitrary size hyperedges (representing carpools) arrive
online, from Pwhich one person must be designated the driver; the “fair share” [FW83] for person i
is defined as t:i∈et 1/|et |, and we would like to ensure that the number of times any person drives
stays close to this. In another result, [BRS22] show that the approximability of minimizing the
maximum flow time on related machines is, up to O(1) factors, the same as offline edge-weighted
carpooling.
More generally, the problem is also closely related to several other areas such as the power of two-
choices [ABKU94, KP06], chip games [ALS+ 89, BLS91], fairness in scheduling and social-networks
[FW83, AAN+ 98, FKK+ 16], online discrepancy [Bár79, BS20, AT25] and rounding [BRS22, LR26],
and has received a lot of attention.
However, despite much interest, there remains a long-standing and fundamental cube-root versus
square-root gap for the problem — both in the deterministic and the stochastic setting.

Deterministic Setting. Here we consider online carpooling where the algorithm is deterministic
and the arrivals are adversarial. In their seminal work, Ajtai et al. [AAN+ 98] showed that the
natural Greedy algorithm, that orients each arriving edge towards the endpoint with smaller dis-
crepancy, achieves an O(T 1/2 ) discrepancy after T requests.1 On the other hand, they show that any
deterministic online algorithm must also incur at least Ω(T 1/3 ) discrepancy, provided that T ≤ n3 .
Coppersmith et al. [CNP+ 11] further showed that Greedy is optimal for this problem. However,
the asymptotic bound on the discrepancy achieved by Greedy had remained unclear.
There are two key conceptual reasons for this gap. First, both the lower and upper bounds of
[AAN+ 98] are based on tracking the standard quadratic potential ∥dt ∥22 . But using this to control
∥dt ∥∞ is inherently lossy and cannot bridge the T 1/3 versus T 1/2 gap. Second, there is a simple
Ω(T 1/2 ) lower bound for arbitrary 2-sparse vectors vt of the form a1(i) + b1(j) with a, b ∈ [−1, 1].
Thus, any sub-square-root upper bound must exploit the additional “combinatorial structure” of

Stochastic Setting. Here we assume that there is an underlying graph G and at each time step,
the request is an edge of G sampled uniformly at random. Ajtai et al. [AAN+ 98] considered the case
where G is the complete graph Kn , and showed that the expected discrepancy is O(log log n) at any
time; analogous to the power of two-choices phenomenon in load balancing [ABKU94, BCSV00].
The harder setting of general graphs G was studied by Gupta et al. [GKKS20], who gave an algorithm
with polylog(T ) discrepancy. They assume that G is known to the algorithm, and use expander
decomposition techniques together with the ideas of [PTW15] to obtain the bound. Currently, the
best known bound for general graphs is O(log1/2 T ), which follows from the breakthrough, and
more general, results of Kulkarni, Reis, and Rothvoss [KRR24] and Aden-Ali [AA26] on online
discrepancy.
On the other hand, the best lower bound is Ω(log1/3 T ) [AAN+ 98], which follows directly from
the deterministic Ω(T 1/3 ) lower bound above: roughly, the random process has sufficiently many
independent opportunities that one block of Ω(log T ) arrivals realizes the equal-discrepancy choices
used by the deterministic lower-bound adversary. Closing this log1/3 T versus log1/2 T gap has been
a natural open question, see e.g., Conjecture 2 in [KRR24].


1.1   Our results

Our first result completely resolves the deterministic case by showing that the Greedy algorithm
itself matches the lower bound of Ajtai et al. [AAN+ 98].
Theorem 1.1 (Deterministic upper bound). For any sequence of T edge arrivals with weights
wt ∈ [0, 1] on a graph on n vertices, the Greedy algorithm satisfies

                                   max ∥dt ∥∞ = O min{T 1/3 , n} .

This cube-root upper bound for carpooling is perhaps surprising given the Ω(T 1/2 ) lower bound for
2-sparse vectors, mentioned previously. Indeed, our analysis will crucially exploit that vectors have
the form wt (1(i) − 1(j)).
A key idea is to track a hierarchy of top-r potentials that captures the entire profile of the discrepancy
vector dt , and use this to prove a refined structural property that we call spreadness. Roughly, this
says that if the discrepancy ever reaches D at some time t, then at some previous time t′ ≤ t, there
must be a set of Ω(D) coordinates whose average absolute discrepancy is Ω(D). This forces the
quadratic potential at that earlier time to be Ω(D3 ); since Greedy’s quadratic potential is always
O(T ), the desired D = O(T 1/3 ) bound follows.

nomenon holds for regular graphs with T = O(n) requests.

To show that such a set R must exist at some earlier time, we simultaneously track, for every r,
the top-r sum Sr (t), defined as the sum of the r largest signed discrepancies at time t. However,
one serious difficulty is that the mass need not be spread at all at any given time. E.g., even if the
mass is currently spread, the adversary can give new requests to bring all coordinates back close to
0, except the one with the largest value (leaving the discrepancy unchanged).
To handle this we prove a stronger inductive property than spreadness. We track the evolution of
the Sr (t) over time. We show that if a particle reaches high discrepancy at time T , then, for every
r, there is a time tr ≤ T at which Sr (tr ) crosses a suitably-chosen threshold. The existence of such
times where Sr is large is proved via a careful backward induction over time.

Stochastic arrivals. We now describe the key ideas for Theorem 1.2. By dividing the T = O(n)
requests into O(1) batches of size cn for c ≪ 1, it suffices to prove the bound for such a batch.
Let Gsamp denote the sampled multigraph, formed by the cn sampled requests. A standard witness
tree argument shows that w.h.p. every connected component C of Gsamp has O(log n) vertices and
O(log n) edges. As different components do not interact, one can essentially apply the bound on
Greedy from Theorem 1.1 to each component individually, which immediately gives the O((log n)1/3 )
discrepancy bound.

The stronger bound (1). Proving the stronger bound given by (1) is more subtle, and requires
two additional key ideas.
First, adapting the witness-tree framework of Kenthapadi and Panigrahy [KP06], we show that
when ∆ is large, every connected component C of Gsamp is nearly a tree. In particular, if we view
C as consisting of a spanning tree with m − 1 edges plus p surplus internal edges, then w.h.p. we
have m = O(log n) and p = O(log n/ log ∆).
Second, we exploit this structure and prove a stronger O(p1/3 + log m) bound for such tree-like
adversarial request sequences, in contrast to the general O((m + p)1/3 ) bound from Theorem 1.1.
Notice that the cube-root dependence now is only on the surplus edges p, while the dependence on
m is logarithmic.
To prove this refined bound, we analyze the effect of edge arrivals differently depending on whether
they merge two different components, or are already inside some component. To show that only the
internal edges incur the cube-root dependence, a key idea is to consider another auxiliary carpooling
instance with one extra vertex, where the discrepancies of the original vertices are shifted by the
logarithm of the current size of the component containing them. We can then use the analysis for
Theorem 1.1 on this instance and infer the refined bound. We refer to Section 4 for details.

The Greedy algorithm and chip view. We will only consider the Greedy algorithm, which,
upon the request at time t, if dt−1 (it ) ≤ dt−1 (jt ), chooses ϵt so that:

                      dt (it ) = dt−1 (it ) + wt   and   dt (jt ) = dt−1 (jt ) − wt .              (3)

It will be convenient to view the discrepancies as n chips on the real line, one for each coordinate,
where each chip i is at position dt (i) at the end of time t. In this chip view, each request picks two
chips, and Greedy moves the left chip by wt to the right, and the right chip by wt to the left.
The question is: how far can an adversary push any chip from the origin?

Expanding and contracting moves. For the purpose of our analysis, we will distinguish two
types of chip moves by Greedy. Consider time t and let i, j denote the requested chips so that
dt−1 (i) ≤ dt−1 (j). A move is expanding if dt (i) > dt−1 (j), and contracting otherwise. In words,
a move is expanding if chip i moves to the right of chip j’s previous position. Equivalently, an
expanding move increases the distance between the two chips i and j. See Figure 1.

4    Stochastic Arrivals from Regular Graphs

We now consider the stochastic setting and prove Theorem 1.2.
Recall that here we are given a ∆-regular base graph G = ([n], E), and at each time t, the request
is an edge et = (ut , vt ), sampled independently and uniformly from E. For ease of exposition, we
assume that each request has weight 1, and we use the words request and edge interchangeably.
However, the bound in Theorem 1.2 holds for arbitrary weights wt ∈ [0, 1], even when wt is chosen
adaptively based on the previously sampled edges e1 , . . . , et−1 , as we will reduce the analysis of the
stochastic setting to a suitable deterministic instance and apply Theorem 1.1.

Sampled Graph. As discussed in Section 1.2, we can assume that T = cn, and we set c = 1/16.
The key object in the proof of Theorem 1.2 is the sampled multigraph Gsamp , which has vertex set [n],
and edge-multiset e1 , . . . , ecn consisting of edges sampled independently from G (with multiplicity).
The proof has two ingredients: First, we show that Gsamp has connected components of size O(log n),
and that each component is close to a tree. As Greedy works on each component of Gsamp (formed
in hindsight) separately, we can apply the adversarial bound in Theorem 1.1 to the request sequence
within each component. This already gives the O(log1/3 n) bound in Theorem 1.2. Second, to prove
the stronger bound (1) we prove a more refined adversarial bound for Greedy when the multigraph
formed by the requests is tree-like.

Proof of Theorem 1.2. Condition on the event in Lemma 4.1, and fix a component C of Gsamp .

4.1   Greedy on Tree-Like Request Sequences

Proof of Lemma 4.2. For 0 ≤ t ≤ T , let Ht be the multigraph formed by the first t requests, and let
ct (u) denote the size of the component containing u in Ht . We call a request ρt = (u, v) merging if
u and v lie in different components of Ht−1 , and internal otherwise. Since H is connected, exactly
m − 1 requests are merging, and hence exactly p are internal.
The idea is to absorb the effect of merging requests into the growth of the component sizes, so that
only the p internal requests can contribute expanding moves. Define

                   xt (u) := dt (u) − 2 log2 ct (u),        yt (u) := −dt (u) − 2 log2 ct (u).

We show that xt (u) ≤ 4p1/3 for every u and t. The same argument applied to yt gives the analogous
bound. Since ct (u) ≤ m, it then follows that

                                        |dt (u)| ≤ 4p1/3 + 2 log2 m.

To bound xt (u), consider an auxiliary chip game with one chip for each vertex u and one dummy
chip z, initially placed sufficiently far to the left of the origin. All the vertex chips start at the

                                                       10
origin. The idea is to realize the evolution of xt so that every internal request in Ht is simulated
by a single chip move in the auxiliary game, while every merging request is simulated using only
contracting moves. The auxiliary game then has at most p expanding moves, and Lemma 3.1 gives
the desired bound.
Suppose first that ρt = (u, v) is internal, and label its endpoints so that dt−1 (u) ≤ dt−1 (v). Since u
and v are in the same component, ct−1 (u) = ct−1 (v), and hence xt−1 (u) ≤ xt−1 (v). Moreover, the
component sizes do not change, so
                            xt (u) = xt−1 (u) + 1,        xt (v) = xt−1 (v) − 1.
Thus the update is exactly one chip move of weight 1.
Now suppose that the request ρt = (u, v) merges components A and B containing u and v respec-
tively. Let a = |A| and b = |B|, and suppose dt−1 (u) ≤ dt−1 (v). As dt−1 (u) ≤ dt−1 (v), we have
dt (u) = dt−1 (u) + 1 and dt (v) = dt−1 (v) − 1, and thus
                       xt (u) = xt−1 (u) + 1 − α,         xt (v) = xt−1 (v) − 1 − β,
where α := 2 log2 ((a + b)/a) and β := 2 log2 ((a + b)/b). Note that both α, β > 0.
Moreover, every chip in A \ {u} decreases by α, and every chip in B \ {v} decreases by β.
The dummy chip z serves simply as a sink for these decreases. By placing it sufficiently far to
the left, we can decrease any vertex chip by an arbitrary amount using contracting moves with z,
splitting the decrease into moves of weight at most 1. Thus all updates for vertices other than u
and v are immediate.
It remains to handle u and v. If α ≥ 1, both chips move left (note that v always moves to the left
as β > 0), and we again use z. Suppose therefore that α < 1, and let w := 1 − α ∈ (0, 1). So chip
u moves right by w.
A key observation is that this move is contracting. To this end we will show that xt−1 (v)−xt−1 (u) ≥
w. Indeed,
           xt−1 (v) − xt−1 (u) = dt−1 (v) − dt−1 (u) + 2 log2 (a/b) ≥ 2 log2 (a/b) = β − α.
                                       √
Now α < 1 implies that (a + b)/a ≤ 2 and hence that a > b. But this implies that β > 2, and
thus β − α ≥ 2 − α > 1 − α = w.
We first make a weight-w move on the chips u, v, which is contracting. This moves u to its required
position xt (u) and decreases v by w. The remaining decrease required at v is 1 + β − w = α + β > 0,
which can again be carried out using z.
Thus every merging request can be simulated entirely by contracting moves, whereas each internal
request contributes at most one expanding move. Since there are exactly p internal requests, Lemma
3.1 gives xt (u) ≤ 4p1/3 for every u and t. Applying the same argument to yt completes the proof.
